1The robot

Two pumps between the bottles and the vial

Each pump has a syringe that holds 5 mL and a valve with six ports. Each port has a tube to one thing: a dye bottle, clean water, the air line, the vial, or the waste bottle.

The robot does one kind of move: turn a pump's valve to a port, then move its plunger. Pull the plunger up and liquid comes in through that port. Push it down and the liquid goes out the same way.

    3The know-how

    What the LLMs didn't know, written down

    An LLM has read a lot about chemistry, but it has never seen our robot. It doesn't know that only one pump is connected to the air line, that a syringe holds only 5 mL, or that the vial must be stirred with bubbles.

    So we wrote the lab's know-how down for it, in a form a computer can read. Computer scientists call this an ontology. It is built from four kinds of entry:

    Categoriesthe kinds of thing: pump, port, bottle
    Definitionswhat each part is: pump 1's syringe holds 5 mL
    Relationshipshow parts connect: port 4 goes to the air line
    Good practicewhat a good job means: stir with air at the end

    We wrote it in OWL, a standard format that people and computers can both read. Step through how it is built.

    4The chemistry

    The same know-how explains how colours mix

    Each dye lets some colours of light through and blocks others. Red dye blocks green and blue light. In a mix, the light blocked by each dye adds up, in proportion to how much of that dye there is.

    7Which form

    Does the know-how have to be in a computer's language?

    We gave the LLMs the same jobs with the know-how in five forms: nothing at all; the ontology itself, written in Turtle, a language for computers; every one of its facts as a plain English sentence; only its good-practice rules; and a short fact sheet for the job, written by a reasoner, a program that works out what follows from the ontology (which pump can reach the air line, for example).

    Why it matters

    Write the lab's knowledge down once, and an LLM can use it

    Labs run on knowledge that lives in people's heads: which tube goes where, what you must never do, how much a syringe holds. Written down so a computer can read it, as an ontology, that know-how can be used by people and LLMs alike, checked automatically, and updated in one place when the lab changes.

    If plain English works as well, why write the know-how in a computer's language? Because programs can use it too. In our test the plain-English version and the reasoner's fact sheet were both made from the ontology, and when a tube moves, every version changes with it.

    ColourMix is a small, safe version of a big question for automated labs: can an LLM be trusted to drive real equipment? On their own, even the strongest LLMs made mistakes a technician never would. With the lab's know-how, the ones that think before answering stopped making them.
